# Verifying Claims

## Overview
Most bad citations are not invented. They are real-looking links to real institutions that don't support the sentence they're attached to. Invented statistics are easy to catch and rare; a genuine finding quoted one notch too strongly is neither. This skill is the check that catches the second kind.

## When to Use
- Any draft containing numbers, percentages, study names, or attributed quotes
- Before publishing, sending, or committing prose that cites anything
- Reviewing a draft that cites research — including your own from earlier in the session

## The Four Checks

### 1. Trace to the primary source
For each claim, find the publication of the organization being credited. Three failure shapes to expect:

**The aggregator.** A "50 statistics about X" roundup. These lift numbers without methodology, mutate them over time, and cite each other. Replace with the institution's own page.

**The vendor blog.** A company selling the product the research endorses, citing a study as "(Institution, Year)" with no link. Search the institution's own site. If nothing there matches, the number does not exist for your purposes — cut it. Do not launder it by citing the vendor as though it were the institution.

**The wrong page.** The link resolves, the page is real, and the claim isn't on it. Fetch and read any link you didn't place yourself.

A missing number costs the draft less than a broken citation costs the author.

### 2. Compare the source's wording to the draft's
This is where most real failures live. Read the actual sentence in the source, then read the sentence in the draft.

Watch for:
- A finding about a **group** narrowed to one member of it ("three noncash motivators rated higher" → "praise alone rated higher")
- A **hedged** result reported flat ("no less or even more effective" → "stronger")
- A **range** reported as its top end ("up to 45%" → "45%")
- A **qualified** population dropped ("employees receiving high-quality recognition" → "employees who get recognized")
- A **correlation** written as a cause

### 3. One link per finding
Two adjacent numbers from the same research partnership are often from different studies published years apart. Check report names and publication dates. If they differ, each number needs its own link, and the prose needs a "separately" or "a later study" so the reader isn't sent to one page expecting both.

### 4. Does the number measure the claim?
The check that survives all the others, and the easiest to skip: a statistic can be real, correctly quoted, and correctly linked, and still be the wrong evidence.

A survey asking which recognition people found *most memorable* does not measure how *much* recognition each source produces. If the argument is about volume or capacity, that number cannot carry it. Ask: what did the researchers actually measure, and is that the thing this sentence needs? If not, the argument may still be sound on its own logic — let it stand alone rather than propping it up with a number that doesn't fit.

## Quotations
- **Splices need ellipses.** Two passages from different parts of a source joined into one blockquote read as continuous text. Mark the seam.
- **Insertions need brackets.** Rewriting a fragment into a grammatical sentence is fine when bracketed.
- **Don't silently normalize.** Capitalization, spelling, and punctuation inside a quote stay as the source has them. Fixing them is fine; fixing them invisibly is not.
- **Count your interventions.** Four brackets and an ellipsis in a two-sentence quote invites the reader to wonder what else changed. Consider two separate blockquotes instead.
- **Verify the surrounding facts.** Book titles drift in personal notes. Author pronouns get guessed constantly — look them up, or write the sentence so it doesn't need one.

## Search Order
For anything the author has read, search their own notes before the web. Highlight archives hold verbatim passages with chapter context that public search often cannot surface at all. Web search is for third-party data and for confirming what the notes already claim.

A quote the author captured themselves but that has no public source is still usable: attribute it by name and leave it unlinked. That is better than dropping real material, and better than hedging it into "some leaders say."

## Funded Research: Know It, Don't Necessarily Print It
When a study is co-branded with a company that sells the thing it endorses, **surface it to the author every time** — that is what the check is for. Whether it reaches the page is the author's call, not the checker's.

The non-negotiable part is the citation: right source, right numbers, right strength of claim. A disclosure clause is one option on top of that, and it can disarm the reader most likely to go checking. It is not required, and a post that discloses every funding relationship it touches reads like a compliance filing rather than an argument. Report the conflict, recommend if you have a view, then let the author decide.

## Verdicts
| Finding | Action |
|---|---|
| No primary source exists | Cut the claim |
| Real source, overstated in the draft | Reword to match what was published |
| Real claim, wrong or aggregated link | Re-source to the primary |
| Two findings, one link | Split into two links, signal the separation in prose |
| Real and correctly cited, but wrong evidence for this claim | Keep the number and fix the claim, or let the argument stand without it |
| Real, correctly cited, vendor-funded | Keep. Report the funding to the author; whether to disclose in the prose is their call |
